Federal tax isn't the only bill a big lottery win creates — your state usually wants a share too,
and how much varies more than most people expect. A handful of states take nothing at all; a
couple take close to 11% at the top end. Here's how it breaks down, and why the number withheld
at the counter isn't always the full story.

## The states that charge nothing

Two different groups end up at 0%, for different reasons:

**States that run a lottery and simply don't tax the winnings:** Delaware, Florida, New
Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming.

**California is a special case** — it otherwise taxes income up to 13.3%, one of the highest
rates in the country, but state law specifically exempts California Lottery winnings from that
tax. If you win big playing a California-sold ticket, that exemption applies regardless of your
overall income.

**A separate group of states don't have their own lottery at all** — Alabama, Alaska, Hawaii,
Nevada, and Utah. You'll sometimes see these lumped into "no tax on lottery winnings" lists, which
is technically true but a bit beside the point: there's no in-state lottery to win in the first
place. If you live in one of these states but buy a multi-state ticket (Powerball or Mega
Millions) while traveling elsewhere, the tax treatment follows the state where you bought and
claimed the ticket — not your home state. See our
[how to claim a lottery prize guide](/guides/how-to-claim-a-lottery-prize/) for more on how
location governs the claims process generally.

## Every other state, grouped by rate

These figures reflect the rate most commonly reported for lottery winnings at the state level.
A caveat before you rely on any of them for a real decision: state tax reporting on lottery
winnings is genuinely inconsistent across sources — some report the flat rate withheld at the
time you claim, others report the top marginal income-tax bracket a large win would land you in,
and those two numbers aren't always the same (more on why below). Treat this as a strong starting
point, not a substitute for checking your specific state's current rate.

**Highest (roughly 8% and up):** District of Columbia and Maryland (8.95%), Oregon (8%), and New
York and New Jersey — both widely reported at the very top of this list, with figures we found
ranging from roughly 8% up to New Jersey's well-documented 10.75% top bracket and New York's
10.9% top bracket (New York City residents can face an additional local tax on top of that).
Given how much these two specifically varied across sources, confirm the current rate directly
with a tax professional before treating either figure as exact.

**Around 6–7.7%:** Wisconsin (7.65%), Minnesota (7.25%), South Carolina (7%), Connecticut
(6.99%), Idaho (6.93%), Montana (6.9%), West Virginia (6.5%).

**Around 5–6%:** Vermont (6%), Rhode Island (5.99%), Arkansas and New Mexico (5.9%), Georgia
(5.75%), North Carolina (5.25%), and a large cluster at almost exactly 5%: Iowa, Kansas,
Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Massachusetts, Mississippi, Nebraska, and Oklahoma.

**Around 4–5%:** Illinois (4.95%), Arizona and Ohio (4.8%), Michigan (4.25%), Colorado, Missouri,
and Virginia (4%).

**Under 4%:** Indiana (3.23%), Pennsylvania (3.07%), and North Dakota — the lowest rate among
states that tax lottery winnings at all, at 2.9%.

## Why the withheld amount isn't always your final bill

This is the same dynamic already covered in our
[federal lottery tax guide](/guides/how-lottery-winnings-are-taxed/), just applied at the state
level: many states withhold tax at a flat rate the moment you claim a prize, but that withholding
is a deposit toward what you actually owe, not a final number. Your real liability is calculated
against your total taxable income for the year when you file, using your state's actual bracket
structure. For a jackpot-sized win, that almost always means the flat withholding rate and your
true liability land close together (a win that large typically pushes you into the top bracket
either way) — but for a smaller prize, or in a state with a graduated rate structure, the two
numbers can genuinely diverge. This is exactly why sources sometimes report different-looking
numbers for the same state: one is quoting the withholding rate, another the top marginal
bracket.

## What this actually means in dollars

State tax differences add up fast at jackpot scale. On a hypothetical $1 billion advertised
jackpot, the difference between claiming in a 0%-tax state like Texas and a high-tax state like
New York works out to roughly $51,230,000 in state tax alone — before factoring in the federal
tax that applies everywhere regardless of which state you're in. That gap alone is larger than
most lottery jackpots ever awarded, which is part of why some winners specifically weigh where a
ticket was purchased when multiple options are realistically available (for multi-state games,
you can only claim in the state where you actually bought the ticket — this isn't a choice you
can make after the fact).

## A second dollar example, at a more realistic jackpot size

Billion-dollar jackpots get the headlines, but they're rare — most jackpot wins are smaller.
Scaling the same math down to a more common $100 million win: the state-tax difference between a
0%-tax state and a roughly 10%-tax state works out to around $10 million on the full advertised
amount, or closer to $5 million if you're comparing the after-lump-sum cash values instead. The
underlying principle holds at any size — state tax location matters proportionally, not just at
the billion-dollar extreme.

## Consider talking to a tax professional before you owe quarterly payments

One detail that's easy to miss: a large lottery win can push you into needing to make quarterly
estimated tax payments for the rest of that tax year, on top of whatever was withheld at the time
you claimed. This applies at the federal level and, depending on your state, potentially at the
state level too. Underpaying throughout the year — even if you eventually pay the right amount
when you file — can trigger an underpayment penalty. This is exactly the kind of detail a tax
professional will catch that's easy to miss if you're only thinking about the one-time withholding
taken out at claim time.

## Don't forget federal tax on top of all of this

Every figure above is state tax only. Federal withholding (24% at the time of the win, though
your actual federal liability for a large prize is typically closer to the top 37% bracket) still
applies no matter which state you're in — see our
[full breakdown of federal lottery tax](/guides/how-lottery-winnings-are-taxed/) for how that
works, plus how the lump-sum-vs-annuity choice affects your total tax picture over time.

## The bottom line

Where you buy and claim a winning ticket has a real, sometimes enormous, effect on your final tax
bill — not just your home state's income tax situation. If you're anywhere near a state line and
considering where to buy a ticket for a specific drawing, or you've already won and are deciding
how to claim, get state-specific advice from a tax professional before you do anything else. The
numbers above are a starting point for understanding the landscape, not a substitute for that
conversation.
