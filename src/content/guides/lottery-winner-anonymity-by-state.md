"Can I stay anonymous?" is one of the first questions almost every jackpot winner asks, and it's
also one of the messiest to answer with a single confident list — state rules genuinely differ,
sources genuinely disagree on the edges, and the rules change as legislatures act. Here's what we
found, including where our own research didn't fully agree.

## The short answer

It depends entirely on the state where the winning ticket was bought and claimed — not where you
live. Roughly a third to half of states offer some form of anonymity or a trust/LLC workaround,
but the exact list, and the conditions attached to it, vary enough that "check your specific
state directly" is genuinely the most accurate answer we can give before you rely on it for a
real decision.

## States consistently reported as allowing anonymity

Across every source we cross-checked, the same core group came up every time: **Delaware,
Kansas, Maryland, Missouri, Montana, New Jersey, North Dakota, South Carolina, and Wyoming.** If
you're trying to get a sense of which states most reliably protect winner privacy, this is the
list we'd put the most weight on.

## States reported as allowing anonymity by some sources, not others

Here's where it gets genuinely inconsistent. A second group of states showed up as
anonymity-friendly in some of the reporting we reviewed but not all of it: **Arizona, Georgia,
Mississippi, Texas, Virginia, and West Virginia.** A third, more thinly sourced group — Arkansas,
Illinois, Kentucky, Minnesota, Ohio, and Oregon — appeared in only one of the sources we checked.

We're showing you this breakdown instead of just picking one list, because that's a more honest
reflection of the actual state of public information here than presenting false confidence would
be. If your state falls into either of these two groups, treat it as "possibly, but confirm
directly" rather than a settled yes.

## States with partial anonymity

A few states occupy a middle ground: **Colorado, Florida, and Michigan** are reported to allow
some limited protection — for example, withholding a home address while still confirming a name,
or offering anonymity only above a certain prize threshold — rather than either full disclosure
or full anonymity.

## States that generally require public disclosure

States commonly reported as requiring the winner's name (and often city) to be made public
include California, Connecticut, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Louisiana, Maine, Massachusetts,
Nebraska, New Hampshire, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania,
Rhode Island, South Dakota, Tennessee, Vermont, Washington, and Wisconsin. Even here, though, a
trust or LLC claim structure (below) is often still available as a partial workaround.

## States without a state lottery at all

Alabama, Alaska, Hawaii, Nevada, and Utah don't run their own lotteries, so this question doesn't
directly apply to tickets bought in-state. A resident of one of these states who wins by buying a
Powerball or Mega Millions ticket while traveling is still governed by the rules of the state
where that ticket was actually purchased — not their home state.

## The trust or LLC workaround

Even in a state that legally requires disclosure of the winner's name, many still allow the prize
to be claimed through a properly formed trust or LLC rather than as an individual. The claim
itself, and the entity's name, is usually still a public record — but your personal name may not
have to be, depending on how the entity and its trustee are structured. This is not a same-day
decision: setting up a trust correctly, before you claim, generally requires an attorney, and
doing it incorrectly (or too late) can undermine the privacy benefit entirely. If anonymity
matters to you and you're in a disclosure state, this is worth arranging *before* you sign
anything, not after — see our
[how to claim a lottery prize guide](/guides/how-to-claim-a-lottery-prize/) for the broader
sequence of what to do first.

## Steps that can help even in a disclosure state

If you're required to be publicly named, there are still practical steps that can reduce your
overall exposure, beyond the trust/LLC structure covered above. Winners who've gone through this
process publicly have commonly taken steps like getting a new, unlisted phone number, reviewing
and locking down social media privacy settings before any announcement, avoiding publicly
confirming a new home address, and in some cases hiring a security consultant for a period of
time after the win becomes public. None of these change what's legally required to be disclosed,
but they can meaningfully reduce unwanted contact once your name is out there.

## Why some winners choose to go public even when they don't have to

Not every winner who has the option to stay anonymous takes it. Some choose to go public
specifically to control the narrative on their own terms — doing one organized press event tends
to generate less ongoing attention than an anonymous winner whose identity leaks out later. Others
cite wanting to be able to publicly support family, a hometown, or a charitable cause without
having to hide the source of the money. There's no universally right choice here — it's a genuine
tradeoff between privacy and control that depends on your own circumstances and comfort level.

## Prize thresholds matter too

Several anonymity-friendly states only extend that protection above a certain prize amount —
reported thresholds we found ranged widely, commonly somewhere between $250,000 and several
million dollars, depending on the state. A modest win in an otherwise anonymity-friendly state
may still be subject to disclosure if it falls under that state's specific threshold. This is
another detail worth confirming directly rather than assuming applies uniformly.

## Why this list keeps changing

Legislatures have actively revisited lottery-winner privacy rules in recent years — some states
have added anonymity protections that didn't exist a few years ago, and the reverse has also
happened. That's a meaningful part of why sources disagree on the edges of this list: they were
often accurate when written, and the underlying law has simply moved since then. Treat any list
you find online, including this one, as a starting point for a conversation with an attorney, not
a final answer.

## The bottom line

If privacy matters to you, don't guess based on a list you found online — confirm the current
rule for the specific state where you bought your ticket, ideally with an attorney, before you
sign the back of anything or make any public statement. The core group of consistently-reported
anonymity states (Delaware, Kansas, Maryland, Missouri, Montana, New Jersey, North Dakota, South
Carolina, and Wyoming) is the closest thing to a reliable starting point we found — everything
past that requires direct confirmation.
