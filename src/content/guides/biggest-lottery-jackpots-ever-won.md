Jackpot size is the number everyone remembers, but the full picture — who won, what they chose,
and what actually happened next — is more interesting than the headline figure alone. Here's a
verified rundown of the largest US lottery jackpots on record.

## #1: The $2.04 billion Powerball, November 2022

Still the largest lottery jackpot ever awarded in the US. The winning ticket was sold at a gas
station in Altadena, California, and the winner, Edwin Castro, came forward publicly and chose
the lump-sum cash option — about $997.6 million before taxes, roughly half the advertised
annuity figure, which is typical for the cash-vs-annuity gap on a jackpot this size. See our
[cash option vs. annuity guide](/guides/lottery-cash-option-vs-annuity/) for why that roughly
50% haircut is normal, not a bad deal specific to this drawing.

## #2: The $1.817 billion Powerball, December 2025

The second-largest jackpot in history, drawn on Christmas Eve 2025, with the winning ticket sold
in Arkansas. The winner chose the lump-sum option, reported at roughly $834.9 million before
taxes, and elected to remain private rather than doing a public reveal — a choice that depends
entirely on Arkansas's specific rules on winner disclosure. See our
[lottery winner anonymity guide](/guides/lottery-winner-anonymity-by-state/) for how that decision
works state by state.

## #3 and #4: The Mega Millions and Powerball records that came before

The $1.602 billion Mega Millions jackpot (August 2023, Neptune Beach, Florida) held the Mega
Millions record until the December 2025 Powerball surpassed it in the all-time ranking. Its
winner also took the lump sum, reported at approximately $794.2 million before taxes. Before
that, the January 2016 $1.586 billion Powerball drawing remains the only jackpot on this list
split between multiple winning tickets — three, sold in California, Tennessee, and Florida —
each entitled to an equal share of the prize.

## #5 and #6: Two jackpots, two very different disclosure choices

The October 2018 $1.537 billion Mega Millions jackpot was claimed by a single winner in South
Carolina — one of the states where anonymity is available — who chose to remain unidentified and
reportedly directed a portion of the winnings to charity. The January 2023 $1.35 billion Mega
Millions jackpot winner also chose not to be publicly identified. Both cases are a reminder that
even at record-setting jackpot size, staying private is possible where state law allows it —
publicity is a choice, not a requirement, in every state that permits anonymous claims.

## The rest of the top 10

Rounding out the list: a $1.05 billion Mega Millions jackpot (January 2021, Michigan), a $768.4
million Powerball jackpot (March 2019, Wisconsin), a $758.7 million Powerball jackpot (August
2017, Massachusetts), and a $731.1 million Powerball jackpot (January 2021, Maryland). All four
remain well inside the top 10 all-time list even years later — a sign of just how rare a
billion-dollar-plus jackpot actually is relative to the merely-enormous ones.

## What these jackpots have in common

A few patterns hold across nearly every entry on this list: almost every publicly documented
winner chose the lump-sum cash option over the annuity, even though it meant taking roughly
half the advertised headline figure. Multi-hundred-million and billion-dollar jackpots
overwhelmingly go to single winning tickets rather than being split, simply because the odds of
two people matching all six numbers in the same drawing are extraordinarily low. And where state
law allowed it, a meaningful share of the biggest winners chose to stay private rather than do a
public reveal — see our full breakdown of [how lottery winnings are taxed](/guides/how-lottery-winnings-are-taxed/)
and [what happens if no one wins the jackpot](/guides/what-happens-if-no-one-wins-the-jackpot/)
for more on how a jackpot gets this large in the first place.

## How a jackpot gets this large in the first place

Every jackpot this size follows the same underlying mechanic: when nobody matches all the
numbers in a drawing, the prize doesn't reset — it rolls over and adds to the next drawing's pool,
funded by ticket sales from that following drawing. Both Powerball and Mega Millions have such
long odds against matching every number (1 in 292,201,338 for Powerball, 1 in 290,472,336 for
Mega Millions) that long winless streaks are common, and each rollover compounds on the last.
Every jackpot on this list is the result of an extended streak of drawings with no winner,
sometimes stretching for months. See our
[guide to what happens when nobody wins the jackpot](/guides/what-happens-if-no-one-wins-the-jackpot/)
for the full mechanics of how a rollover works drawing to drawing, and our
[Powerball vs. Mega Millions odds comparison](/guides/powerball-vs-mega-millions-odds/) for how
the two games' odds actually differ.

## Powerball vs. Mega Millions at the very top

Of the ten jackpots listed above, six were Powerball drawings and four were Mega Millions —
including the top two spots, both Powerball. That split roughly tracks each game's overall
jackpot-growth pattern rather than reflecting a meaningful difference in how "winnable" either
game is; both operate on the same basic rolling-jackpot principle, just with slightly different
odds and starting jackpot sizes. Neither game has a structural advantage that makes billion-dollar
jackpots more or less likely over the long run — it mostly comes down to how many consecutive
drawings happen to go by without a winner.

## A note on sourcing

Jackpot rankings you'll find elsewhere online are frequently out of date — several widely
circulated "biggest jackpots" lists still show the January 2016 $1.586 billion Powerball drawing
in the top spot, without accounting for the three larger jackpots awarded since (November 2022,
August 2023, and December 2025). The ranking above reflects the most current figures we could
verify as of publication; if you're reading this well after 2026, a newer jackpot may have since
broken into this list.
