If you win a major jackpot, the payout decision is one of the first — and most consequential —
choices you'll make, and it has to happen close to claim time. Here's what the two options
actually involve.

## The two options, in plain terms

**Lump sum (cash option):** a single payment, made shortly after you claim, equal to the
discounted present-day cash value of the jackpot. It's the smaller of the two numbers, typically
somewhere around half the advertised jackpot figure, before taxes.

**Annuity:** the full advertised jackpot amount, paid out over roughly three decades — commonly
structured as an initial payment followed by 29 additional annual payments, each one about 5%
larger than the last to help offset inflation over the payout period.

Both are being drawn from the same underlying prize pool. The annuity is that pool paid out over
time; the lump sum is what that same pool is worth if you took all of it today instead, once you
account for the time value of money.

## Why the lump sum is roughly half

This surprises a lot of people, but it isn't a penalty or a "worse deal" — it's a standard
present-value calculation. A dollar paid to you today is worth more than a dollar promised 20
years from now, because today's dollar can be invested and grow in the meantime. The lump sum
reflects that difference: it's what the total 30-year payment stream is actually worth in
today's dollars, based on prevailing interest-rate assumptions at the time of the drawing. The
exact discount varies somewhat by prevailing rates, but "roughly half the advertised figure" is a
reasonable rule of thumb across most recent major jackpots.

## The tax angle

This is one of the biggest practical differences between the two options, and it's covered in
more depth in our [lottery tax guide](/guides/how-lottery-winnings-are-taxed/), but the short
version: a lump sum is taxed as income in the single year you receive it, almost certainly
pushing you into the top federal tax bracket for that year. An annuity spreads the same total
taxable income across 30 years instead, which can — depending on your other income and how tax
brackets shift over time — result in a different total tax outcome than paying it all at once.
Neither option is automatically better on tax grounds alone; it depends on bracket assumptions
over a very long time horizon, which is exactly the kind of projection a tax professional should
run for your specific situation before you decide.

## Reasons winners choose the lump sum

- **Control.** You have the full (after-tax) amount available immediately to invest, pay off
  debt, or allocate however you choose, rather than waiting on a fixed schedule.
- **Investment potential.** If you (or a professional you hire) can realistically achieve
  investment returns above the discount rate baked into the annuity calculation, taking the cash
  and investing it yourself can outperform the annuity over time — though this cuts both ways and
  isn't guaranteed.
- **No 30-year dependency on the lottery commission.** An annuity is a long-term promise from a
  state lottery system; a lump sum removes any reliance on that continuing to be paid out
  reliably over three decades, however unlikely a default might be.
- **It's what most big winners actually do.** The overwhelming majority of publicly documented
  major jackpot winners take the cash option — see our
  [biggest jackpots ever](/guides/biggest-lottery-jackpots-ever-won/) guide for specific,
  confirmed examples.

## Reasons winners choose the annuity

- **Built-in spending discipline.** A fixed annual payment schedule limits how much can be spent
  or lost at once, which financial advisors sometimes recommend specifically as a guard against
  the well-documented pattern of sudden-wealth recipients overspending or being over-persuaded
  into bad investments early on.
- **A predictable, guaranteed income stream** for three decades, without needing to manage a
  large lump sum yourself or pay for ongoing investment management.
- **Potential tax-timing benefits**, in some scenarios — spreading income across many years
  instead of concentrating it all in one top-bracket year, though whether this actually helps
  depends heavily on your specific circumstances and future tax law, which is unknowable in
  advance.

## A worked example at a realistic jackpot size

To make this concrete: on a hypothetical $500 million advertised jackpot, the lump-sum cash
option would typically come in somewhere around $240–260 million, before any tax is withheld —
roughly the same "about half" relationship seen across the real jackpots in our
[biggest jackpots ever won](/guides/biggest-lottery-jackpots-ever-won/) list, though the exact
percentage shifts with prevailing interest rates at the time of the drawing. The annuity option,
by contrast, would pay out the full $500 million over 30 graduated annual installments — starting
smaller in year one and increasing roughly 5% each year to help offset inflation, rather than 30
equal payments of the same size.

## What financial advisors commonly suggest for an average winner

There's no universal rule, but a pattern shows up often enough in financial-planning coverage of
lottery winners to be worth mentioning: for a winner without significant prior experience managing
large sums, several advisors lean toward recommending at least partial protection against
overspending — either through the annuity's built-in pacing, or, for winners who take the lump
sum, deliberately structuring part of it into vehicles that aren't easily accessed on impulse. The
well-documented pattern of sudden-wealth recipients depleting large windfalls faster than expected
is exactly the risk this kind of structuring is meant to guard against. This is general
commentary, not advice tailored to any individual situation — the right structure depends
entirely on your own finances, goals, and risk tolerance, which is why a professional
conversation matters more here than a general rule of thumb.

## This isn't a decision to make on the spot

Every reputable source on this topic agrees on one point: this decision shouldn't be made at the
claims office in the moment. Line up a tax professional and, ideally, a fee-only financial
advisor *before* you formally claim — not after — so the choice is made with real numbers
specific to your situation rather than general rules of thumb like the ones on this page. See our
[how to claim a lottery prize guide](/guides/how-to-claim-a-lottery-prize/) for the fuller
sequence of who to talk to and when, including the anonymity decision that often needs to be
settled around the same time.

## The bottom line

The annuity pays more in total dollars; the lump sum pays less but hands you full control
immediately. Neither is objectively correct — the right choice depends on your investment
outlook, your tax situation over time, and honestly, how confident you are in your own (or your
advisor's) ability to manage a large sum responsibly. Get professional advice specific to your
numbers before deciding, and don't let the size of the headline annuity figure alone drive the
choice.
